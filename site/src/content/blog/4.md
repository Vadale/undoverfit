I cannot quite describe how surprised I was when I found out that my package,
relm, had made it onto CRAN. I had read about the strictness of its technical
checks, and I never expected to build something this ambitious, given what I
knew at the start—especially without knowing either Rust or C++. I got there
with AI assistance. Further down, I will explain how I tried to steer that work.

But before I get too carried away, let me tell you what relm is. It is an
open-source R package for running large language models (LLMs) on your own
computer and working with them through familiar R objects, such as data frames
and matrices.

Alongside generating responses, it lets you observe selected internal
activations—the numerical values produced during computation—compare
conditions and try controlled interventions. The idea is to study a model
using the tools that people working in R already use to analyse data.

## The question that started the project

I do not think scientific research can afford to ignore language models.
I want to understand where they might fit into our work, what they can help
us do and how we can evaluate their contribution.

For me, one question is: where does R belong in all this? I wanted it to have
a more visible role in scientific work with LLMs, building on the statistical
ecosystem researchers already use. That led to two connected ambitions: make
local models accessible from R, and make it possible to study their behaviour
in the same environment.

I want to do research **on** language models and research **with** language
models. These are different goals, but in both cases I want to be able to
examine an experiment's inputs, settings and results.

## Why R, and why local models?

When I think about the value of R, I do not just think about the command used
to fit a regression. I think about the scientific work behind it: specialised
methods, published implementations, diagnostic tools and packages built around
particular research problems. Publications such as
[The R Journal](https://journal.r-project.org/) help connect that software with
the methods and applications it comes from.

That accumulated work is one of the reasons I care about R's future. If a
researcher already has an implementation suited to their problem, an analysis
process they know and the tools to question a result, I would like them to be
able to integrate an LLM into that work.

I also see this code and documentation as a valuable resource for AI-assisted
statistical work. Whether a particular model was trained on those sources,
and can use those methods correctly, still needs to be established. Elegant
R code can apply a technique incorrectly: its assumptions, implementation
and results need checking.

R already has tools for LLMs. For example,
[ellmer](https://ellmer.tidyverse.org/) provides interfaces to different
providers, including local models through Ollama. With relm, I am interested
in bringing local execution together with access to selected internal
activations and model interventions, then bringing the experimental data
into R.

There is also the question of the data. In health research, pharmaceutical
work or other settings involving confidential material, sending text to an
external service can be a problem. Running inference on your own computer
lets you avoid that step in the interaction with the model.

The way you work still needs attention: downloaded software and weights,
file permissions, logs, backups and any connected services are all part of
protecting the data. You also need to choose a model your machine can handle
and check whether it is suitable for the task.

## Researching LLMs, and researching with them

The first goal is to study the model itself. What changes when the input
changes? Which signals can we observe in different layers—the successive
blocks of computation? What happens to the response if we intervene in that
computation?

relm lets you explore these questions on supported models and components.
Activations become data you can compare in R. Steering intervenes on those
activations during execution: it does not retrain the model or rewrite its
weights.

Of course, having a plot of the activations does not mean we have understood
what the model is doing. We are observing part of the computation; making
sense of it requires further checks. Anthropic's
[work on tracing model computations](https://www.anthropic.com/research/tracing-thoughts-language-model)
shows interesting possibilities while acknowledging limits in coverage and
method. relm's tools have their own scope and do not reproduce that
circuit-tracing system.

The second goal is to understand whether a model is useful for a research
task. Imagine using it to extract information from documents before a
statistical analysis. I would want to explain what task it was given, why
that particular model was chosen, which alternatives it was compared with
and how its errors were evaluated.

Internal measurements may help investigate an error or a hypothesis about
behaviour. Judging the result also requires reference data, appropriate
comparisons and an assessment of uncertainty. In health-related work, the
[TRIPOD-LLM reporting guideline](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-024-03425-5)
is a reference for describing how an LLM study was conducted; its checklist
does not certify the quality of the model or study.

That is the direction I would like to take relm: making the LLM's contribution
to research easier to examine. How much it improves a particular analysis,
and whether it is preferable to other models, needs to be demonstrated on
the actual task. The medical and pharmaceutical examples explain a
motivation for the project; they are not validated applications of the package.

## A concrete example: from six notices to a CSV

One starting point is this
[public recipe](https://github.com/Vadale/R-ebirth/tree/main/examples/funding-extraction/quickstart):
extract information from six short funding notices and save it in a CSV for
review. The notices are synthetic: the programmes and amounts are invented,
and there are no personal or clinical records.

![Six synthetic notices pass through a local LLM in R to a CSV for review. In the recorded run, all six amount fields match expectations, while five of six amount-and-quotation pairs match.](/images/posts/relm-funding-workflow.svg)

*The recorded extraction workflow. The CSV keeps the model's mistake visible
for review.*

The task is to find the programme's total budget and a quotation from the
text that supports it. If the total is not stated, both fields must be
`null`, meaning missing. The texts also contain numbers that can confuse
the model, such as a per-project limit or the previous year's funding.

In the run recorded on 23 October 2026, the model returned six complete JSON
responses: six answers with the requested structured fields. The amount
field matches expectations in all six cases, including those where the total
is absent. The amount-and-quotation pairs, however, match in five out of six.

| Notice | Expected total | Amount and quotation |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Cedar | €250,000 | Match |
| Harbour | Not stated | Correct missing amount, incorrect quotation |
| Meadow | €120,000 | Match |
| Riverside | €300,000 | Match |
| Orchard | Not stated | Match |
| Willow | €600,000 | Match |

For Harbour, the model correctly left the amount as `null`, but quoted the
€40,000 limit per project. That number is not the programme's overall budget:
the quotation should also have been `null`. The incorrect response is
preserved in the public results, without automatic repairs or retries.

### Measured time and resources

If you want to try it, it is worth knowing what resources were used. The run
uses relm 0.6.1, jsonlite 2.0.0 and Qwen2.5 1.5B Instruct Q4_K_M, on Linux
with an AMD Ryzen 7 4800H CPU and four configured threads.

- Generating the six responses took approximately **115.5 seconds**.
- Peak resident memory for the R process was **1.245 GiB**, about **1.34 GB**,
  including R, the model and working buffers.
- The model weights occupy about **1.12 GB** on disk.

Generation time excludes downloading and installation. No paid inference
APIs were used; electricity, monetary cost and human-review time were not
measured. These figures describe that single run on that machine.

## What this example shows

The recipe lets you follow the path from documents to structured data and
check every response against the source text. Harbour's error is easy to
miss if you stop at the amount or the response format: the quotation really
appears in the document, but does not support the information being requested.

Six synthetic cases and one run do not tell us how accurate the model will
be on real documents. Even the correct rows still need review in real use;
PDFs, OCR, tables and ambiguous amounts require further evaluation. The
expected answers for the six cases were prepared and checked by the same
assistant that built the recipe, without independent expert evaluation.

The example concerns local extraction. Studying the effect of steering or
other internal operations needs a dedicated experiment. If a response
changes after an intervention, we still have to check whether it improved,
and by what criterion.

## How I worked with AI

I developed relm with AI assistance. My role included defining requirements,
setting priorities and deciding which directions were worth pursuing. Coding
assistants contributed to implementation, debugging, checks and documentation.

In particular, I tried to give direction to what I see as a problem in
research with R. I gave the model information, used my critical judgement
and kept a firm hand on the objectives, while leaving it room to propose
the technical solutions it considered best.

I acted like a product/project manager working with a brilliant engineer
who needs direction on the product to build. I supplied what it needed and
evaluated its work as we went along.

The time I had spent on other projects with LLMs helped. I learned to
recognise when the model was losing its way, could no longer move forward
or was getting stuck in a loop. That experience also led to a skill,
[Assert](https://github.com/Vadale/assert-skill), tailored to my needs and
the way I work, which I will use again. But perhaps that is a story for
another article.

## Trying relm and continuing the conversation

The [complete recipe](https://github.com/Vadale/R-ebirth/tree/main/examples/funding-extraction/quickstart)
contains the data, expected values, prompt, settings, R script and original
responses. You can read the
[recorded results](https://github.com/Vadale/R-ebirth/tree/main/examples/funding-extraction/quickstart/recorded)
without downloading or running the model.

Following the recipe requires R 4.5 or later, relm at least 0.6.1 and
jsonlite 2.0.0. The recorded run uses relm 0.6.1. As of 30 October 2026,
CRAN distributes 0.5.0, while r-universe distributes 0.6.1: check which
version you are using and follow the recipe's instructions.

I would love you to try the package, judge the work and give me feedback
on how to improve it. I am not shying away from criticism: I am a student,
and this is also a project to learn from.

But I would also like to leave you with an invitation. If something in your
workflow keeps getting in the way, if a tool is missing or you would like to
improve the ones you already use, try to see whether an LLM could help you
build it. I managed to create an R package and get it onto CRAN without
knowing Rust or C++. People who know the problems of their own research
well may have very precise ideas about improving their tools, for their
own benefit and for others with the same needs.

You can start without mastering every language involved. You do need
a methodical approach, time to learn and checks on the model's work. Technical experience remains
important, especially when problems arise that we do not yet know how to
recognise or assess. That is a part of the work I am still learning.

While I wait—and hope—for your responses, I would also like to ask: how
are you using these alien intelligences? What criteria do you use to
question and evaluate them, so that you end up with robust, reliable results?

The package page, reference manual and vignettes are available on
[CRAN](https://cran.r-project.org/package=relm). Development sources and
issue tracking are on [GitHub](https://github.com/Vadale/R-ebirth).

Thank you for reading.
